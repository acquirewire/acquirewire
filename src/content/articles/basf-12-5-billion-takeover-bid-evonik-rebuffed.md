## What Happened

BASF, the Ludwigshafen-based chemicals giant, made a takeover approach for Evonik Industries valuing the company at roughly $12.5 billion, according to a Wall Street Journal report. Evonik's board rebuffed the bid. No revised offer has been disclosed, and neither company has confirmed the talks publicly, leaving the situation formally in the "neither confirm nor deny" zone that often precedes either a raised bid or a clean withdrawal.

Evonik, majority-owned by the RAG Foundation, is one of Europe's larger specialty chemicals producers, with operations spanning nutrition, cosmetics ingredients, and high-performance polymers. BASF, by contrast, is a bulk and specialty hybrid that has spent several years shrinking its cost base and divesting non-core businesses. A deal of this scale would be its largest in well over a decade and a sharp strategic pivot back toward consolidation.

## Why It Matters

**The strategic logic is real, even if the price isn't settled.** BASF has been under sustained pressure from high European energy costs, sluggish demand from German manufacturing, and activist-adjacent calls to simplify the portfolio. Acquiring Evonik's specialty businesses would shift BASF's mix toward higher-margin, less energy-intensive chemistry. That argument has been made by analysts for years. The problem is that "strategically logical" and "worth $12.5 billion right now" are different questions, and Evonik's board apparently sees a gap between the two.

**The RAG Foundation's position is the central variable.** RAG holds a majority stake in Evonik and exists specifically to fund the long-term liabilities of Germany's former coal-mining industry. It does not have the same pressure to sell as a typical financial sponsor, and it has historically been protective of Evonik's independence. Any deal that goes through requires RAG's blessing, which gives the foundation significant leverage to demand a premium that a trade buyer might find difficult to swallow. BASF cannot simply go hostile in a meaningful way when the controlling shareholder is a public-interest foundation with no liquidity motive.

**The size alone makes this a market event.** At $12.5 billion, this would rank among the larger chemicals deals globally in recent memory. It would likely trigger antitrust review in the EU, the US, and possibly China, given both companies' international footprints. Remedies could be substantial, particularly in product lines where the two companies overlap in Europe. Financing is a separate question: BASF's balance sheet has been under scrutiny, and a cash deal of this magnitude would meaningfully stretch leverage ratios at a moment when the company is still executing restructuring charges.

    <li><strong>Antitrust complexity:</strong> Overlapping product lines in European specialty chemicals could require significant divestitures, eroding the deal's industrial logic and adding 12-18 months of regulatory uncertainty.</li>
