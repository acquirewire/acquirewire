## What Happened

Eurobank, the Athens-listed lender, has completed its €1 billion acquisition of Fibank, also known as First Investment Bank, vaulting itself to the top of Bulgaria's retail banking market. The deal, confirmed by Reuters, is the most significant cross-border bank acquisition in the Balkans in several years and extends Eurobank's footprint well beyond its Greek home base, where it already holds leading positions in Cyprus and Serbia.

Fibank was one of Bulgaria's last sizeable independently owned banks. Its sale ends a years-long search by the founding Batselovi family for an exit, a process complicated by the bank's historically patchy capital ratios and a 2014 liquidity crisis that required emergency central-bank support. Eurobank's willingness to pay ten-figure euros for the franchise signals a conviction that Bulgaria's EU membership, growing middle class, and still-low banking penetration make the price defensible.

## Why It Matters

**Scale in a fragmented market.** Bulgaria's banking sector is split among a dozen or so players, most of them subsidiaries of larger European groups. First Investment Bank had a material share of retail deposits and SME lending, assets that are expensive to build organically. By acquiring rather than growing branch by branch, Eurobank immediately reaches the top of the asset-size league table in the country and gains a cross-selling base that would have taken a decade to replicate.

**Eurobank's regional consolidation strategy is now clear.** The bank has been methodically acquiring or deepening positions across southeastern Europe, treating the broader region as a single growth engine to offset the maturity and competitive pressure of the Greek domestic market. Bulgaria, an EU member since 2007 and a candidate for eurozone entry, offers a regulatory environment that is familiar to a bank already managing multiple national supervisors. The Fibank deal is not a one-off; it fits a pattern.

**The price carries execution risk.** One billion euros for a bank with Fibank's history is not a bargain entry. Legacy asset-quality problems, the technology gap between the two institutions, and the complexity of absorbing a workforce and culture that operated under family ownership for three decades all add to integration costs that will not appear in the headline number. Bulgaria's central bank, the BNB, will watch the combined entity's capital ratios closely. If loan-book surprises emerge post-integration, the deal economics deteriorate quickly.
