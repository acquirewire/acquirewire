## What Happened

Albertsons is seeking more than $8 billion in damages from Kroger after their proposed $25 billion merger collapsed, according to reporting from the Wall Street Journal and multiple other outlets. The grocery chain argues Kroger failed to take adequate steps to win regulatory approval, leaving Albertsons exposed after years of deal limbo. Kroger, for its part, has disputed that framing. The two companies had agreed to a merger that would have created the largest pure-play grocery operator in the United States, but federal and state antitrust challenges ultimately killed it.

The $8 billion claim is not a rounding error. It is roughly a third of what Kroger agreed to pay for the entire business. Albertsons contends that Kroger dragged its feet on divestitures and remedies that could have satisfied regulators, and that this negligence, or worse, deliberate inaction, caused Albertsons to forgo other strategic options during the period the deal was pending.

## Why It Matters

**The size of the claim signals a new aggression from target companies.** Historically, busted-deal litigation has centered on relatively modest breakup fees negotiated upfront. Here, Albertsons appears to be arguing that the contractual breakup fee is not sufficient compensation for what it lost, and is pursuing broader damages through the courts. If it succeeds, even partially, that outcome would force acquirers to renegotiate how merger agreements handle regulatory risk, particularly in deals that attract serious antitrust scrutiny.

**The regulatory conduct argument is the novel part.** Most busted-deal cases turn on whether a MAC clause was triggered or whether a party simply walked away. This one turns on something harder to quantify: whether Kroger's choices during the regulatory process were competent and made in good faith. Albertsons would need to show that a different divestiture strategy, pursued more aggressively, would have produced a different outcome with the FTC and the state attorneys general who sued to block the deal. That is a high bar, but discovery could be damaging for Kroger regardless of the final verdict.

**The downstream consequences for M&A structuring are real.** Dealmakers are already writing more detailed conduct-of-business covenants into merger agreements when antitrust risk is elevated. A large damages award here would accelerate that trend, likely pushing acquirers toward either more explicit regulatory effort commitments or higher breakup fees at signing, to avoid exactly this kind of open-ended liability.
