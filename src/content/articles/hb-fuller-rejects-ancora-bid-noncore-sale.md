## What Happened

H.B. Fuller's board has rejected the most recent acquisition proposal from Ancora Holdings, the activist investment firm that has been pushing for a sale of the Minneapolis-based adhesives and sealants manufacturer. The company said simultaneously that it plans to review its portfolio and consider selling non-core businesses, a move that reads less like a strategic pivot and more like a concession designed to buy time and demonstrate responsiveness to shareholders without capitulating entirely to Ancora's demands.

The source material does not disclose the price Ancora offered or the specific business units H.B. Fuller has in its sights for divestiture. What is clear is that the board believes the bid undervalues the company, while Ancora evidently disagrees, and this is not the first approach the activist has made.

## Why It Matters

**Ancora is not going away.** The firm has mounted repeated approaches, which signals genuine conviction rather than a speculative punt. Activists who fire off a single low bid and retreat rarely bother to come back; the fact that Ancora has submitted at least one further proposal after an initial rejection tells you it thinks the gap between its offer and the board's valuation can be closed. The question is whether a portfolio review and some asset sales give H.B. Fuller enough of a share price lift to satisfy enough of its investor base and take the wind out of Ancora's campaign.

**A partial divestiture strategy carries its own execution risk.** Selling non-core units sounds tidy in a press release, but it requires finding buyers at prices that justify the disruption, and the industrial adhesives market is not exactly awash with strategic acquirers right now. If the sales process drags or the prices disappoint, H.B. Fuller could find itself having shed assets without meaningfully closing the valuation gap, leaving the stock in a worse position than before and Ancora in a stronger negotiating seat.

**The board's rejection sets up a shareholder vote as the decisive moment.** H.B. Fuller's management will now need to convince investors that its standalone plan, plus whatever proceeds come from non-core disposals, delivers more value than an outright sale. That is a harder argument to make in a period of elevated financing costs, where buyers face real constraints on leverage and deal math is tighter. If Ancora decides to take its case directly to shareholders, either through a proxy fight or a public letter campaign, the board's position becomes considerably more uncomfortable.
