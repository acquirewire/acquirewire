## What Happened

A public hearing has been scheduled for Friday on the proposed $67 billion merger between Dominion Energy and NextEra Energy, according to a report from FOX 5 DC. The hearing marks the first formal public forum where regulators, ratepayers, and other stakeholders can weigh in on a deal that, if completed, would combine two of the largest electric utilities in the United States.

No terms beyond the headline figure have been confirmed in available source material. What is clear is that a $67 billion utility combination would rank among the largest in the sector's history, and that the public hearing process signals the deal has moved far enough along to require serious regulatory engagement.

## Why It Matters

**Scale alone makes this a systemic event.** Dominion serves roughly 3.5 million electric customers across Virginia and the Carolinas. NextEra, through its Florida Power & Light subsidiary and its massive NextEra Energy Resources renewables arm, is already the world's largest generator of wind and solar power. Putting them together would create a utility of unprecedented geographic reach and generation capacity, giving the combined entity significant influence over US grid investment decisions for the next decade.

**Regulators will scrutinize market power and rate impacts.** State utility commissions, particularly Virginia's State Corporation Commission, will be central to whether this deal proceeds. Virginia regulators have historically been protective of Dominion's ratepayers and have not been passive approvers of large restructuring proposals. Friday's hearing is the opening of what will almost certainly be a prolonged and contested review. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission will also need to sign off, and the current administration's posture on utility consolidation is not firmly established.

**Strategic logic cuts both ways.** NextEra would gain Dominion's substantial regulated rate base in the mid-Atlantic and Southeast, regions with strong long-term electricity demand growth driven by data center buildout and industrial reshoring. It would also inherit Dominion's substantial nuclear fleet, a low-carbon asset that has become more valuable as AI-driven power demand reshapes load forecasts. The bull case writes itself. But Dominion has historically run as a politically connected, Virginia-centric operator, and integrating that culture and regulatory relationship into a Florida-headquartered national platform is not straightforward.
