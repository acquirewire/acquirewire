## What Happened

The UK Competition and Markets Authority has told AkzoNobel and Axalta Coating Systems they have one week to offer remedies addressing preliminary competition concerns over their proposed $25 billion merger. The CMA's warning, reported by Reuters and the Wall Street Journal, stops short of a Phase 2 referral for now, but the clock is running. If the parties cannot table acceptable concessions quickly, the deal faces a full, months-long investigation that could materially delay or derail it.

The combination would bring together two of the biggest names in industrial and decorative coatings. AkzoNobel, the Dutch group behind the Dulux brand, agreed to acquire Philadelphia-based Axalta, which is heavily weighted toward automotive refinish and OEM coatings. Together, they would rank among the top two or three coatings companies globally by revenue, alongside Sherwin-Williams and PPG Industries.

## Why It Matters

**Scale is the whole point, and that is precisely the problem.** AkzoNobel and Axalta overlap in several coatings segments, particularly in vehicle refinish, where Axalta has long held a leading position and AkzoNobel is a credible number two in parts of Europe. A merger in that segment concentrates pricing power significantly, which is exactly the kind of structural change the CMA has shown willingness to block or force divestitures to prevent. The regulator's one-week deadline is a signal, not a formality: they want to see concrete asset or business-unit disposals, not vague behavioural commitments.

**The timing adds pressure to both sides.** AkzoNobel has been managing cost pressures and a portfolio restructuring for several years, and Axalta carries meaningful leverage from its private-equity-era capital structure. Neither company benefits from a prolonged regulatory overhang. A Phase 2 referral in the UK would typically add six months or more to the timeline, during which deal certainty erodes, management bandwidth is consumed, and competitors can move. PPG and Sherwin-Williams would have every incentive to pick off customers or talent if the merger drags.

**Remedies in coatings deals are rarely clean.** The most obvious fix, divesting one party's vehicle refinish business in the UK or Europe, is commercially unattractive because those are high-margin franchises with deep distribution relationships. Selling to a credible buyer at a reasonable price is harder than it sounds: the pool of buyers large enough to satisfy the CMA but small enough not to create a new competition problem is thin. If the parties cannot agree with the regulator this week, the Phase 2 path almost certainly involves a divestiture order rather than a voluntary fix, and that shifts negotiating leverage firmly toward the CMA.
