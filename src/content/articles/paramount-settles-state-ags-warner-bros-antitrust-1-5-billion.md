## What Happened

Paramount Skydance has settled the antitrust lawsuit brought by state attorneys general over its acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery, removing the most serious remaining obstacle to a transaction valued at roughly $110 billion. California Attorney General Rob Bonta led the coalition of 12 states — Arizona, California, Colorado, Connecticut, Massachusetts, Minnesota, Nevada, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, Oregon and Washington — that brought the case.

The settlement terms are unusually specific. Paramount has given a five-year, court-enforceable commitment to increase film output: at least an additional $300 million a year on US film production, totalling a minimum of $1.5 billion over five years, and a schedule of 30 movies in each of the first two years rising to 32 in the following three. It will establish a $47.5 million fund for workers affected by the merger, accept restrictions on how it conducts cable negotiations to keep prices competitive, and agree not to sell the Paramount Studios or Warner Bros. lots in California for at least five years. Union lawsuits were resolved alongside the state case.

## Why It Matters

**The remedy is behavioural, not structural.** Antitrust settlements of this size normally involve selling assets. Here the states extracted spending commitments, output quotas and real-estate undertakings instead — effectively trading competition concerns for guaranteed economic activity in California and elsewhere. That is a notable template, and one other state coalitions will study when the next mega-merger lands.

**California folded because the alternative was worse.** The state had positioned itself as the principal obstacle, and Paramount had responded by demanding the states post a bond approaching $1.9 billion to cover delay costs and openly discussing leaving California. A settlement that locks in production spending and preserves the studio lots delivers tangible jobs; a litigated loss would have delivered nothing.

**Closing is now a financing question, not a legal one.** With the states settled and the FCC having already approved foreign-ownership arrangements for the Gulf funds involved, the path is largely clear. Paramount has signalled a roughly $49 billion debt sale to fund the transaction, and David Ellison has told staff the merger could close within weeks — putting execution risk squarely in the credit markets.
