## What Happened

Union Pacific's chief executive is publicly lobbying for an $85 billion combination with Norfolk Southern, a deal that would collapse two of America's seven Class I freight railroads into one and redraw the map of U.S. rail logistics. The pitch is landing in rough terrain. The International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers has become the latest union to formally oppose the merger, adding to a chorus of labor and shipper groups that have raised concerns about pricing power and job security on a combined network that would span roughly half the continent.

The CEO's public campaign is a deliberate move. Surface Transportation Board approval for a deal of this scale would be a multi-year process under rules tightened after the 2000 merger wave, and winning over stakeholders early is standard practice. Whether it is working is another question.

## Why It Matters

**The strategic logic is straightforward, the execution is not.** A UP-NS combination would create a single-line service across the southern and eastern U.S., eliminating interchange friction that costs shippers time and money. For UP, it would also end the structural disadvantage of being locked out of eastern markets. The bull case is essentially about network density: more lanes, fewer handoffs, better asset utilization. Rail M&A historically does produce those gains, eventually.

**Labor opposition is the most immediate political liability.** The IBEW joining existing union opposition matters because the STB weighs public interest broadly, and organized labor has real influence in Washington. Rail unions have not forgotten the 2022 contract dispute that nearly produced a national strike, and they are skeptical that a leaner combined railroad means anything other than fewer jobs. Each new union opponent gives the STB more cover to slow or condition approval, potentially requiring operational concessions that erode the deal's financial rationale.

**Regulatory precedent cuts against large rail mergers.** The STB tightened its public-interest test specifically to make deals like this harder after the problems that followed the 1990s consolidation wave. A transaction combining the first and third largest U.S. freight railroads by revenue would almost certainly trigger an extended review, likely exceeding two years, with mandatory public hearings across multiple states. The agency has broad authority to impose conditions, including trackage rights for competitors, that could dilute synergies substantially. UP's CEO making a press tour is a recognition that the political campaign has to run parallel to any formal filing, not after it.

    <li><strong>Union escalation:</strong> With the IBEW now on record against the deal, other rail unions could follow. Coordinated labor opposition adds political weight and could influence congressional pressure on the STB.</li>
    <li><strong>Norfolk Southern resistance:</strong> There is no confirmed agreement. If NS management and its board view a UP approach as opportunistic or underpriced, a protracted hostile process becomes possible, raising execution costs and uncertainty significantly.</li>

    <li><strong>Freight volume trends favor scale:</strong> Nearshoring and infrastructure spending are pushing more industrial freight onto rail. A larger combined network would be better positioned to win that volume from trucking than either railroad operating separately.</li>
