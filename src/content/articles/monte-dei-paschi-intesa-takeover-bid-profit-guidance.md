## What Happened

Monte dei Paschi di Siena (MPS) has raised its 2026 profit guidance, the bank confirmed this week, with the timing pointed squarely at an expected takeover bid from Intesa Sanpaolo. The move comes after MPS's attempt to acquire Mediobanca collapsed, leaving the Siena-based lender searching for a new strategic path and, in the meantime, trying to anchor its standalone valuation as high as possible before Intesa sets its price.

MPS has spent the past few years clawing its way back from successive state bailouts and a capital structure that once looked unrepairable. The Italian government, still a significant shareholder, has been gradually reducing its stake, and the bank's recovery has been faster than many analysts expected. That rehabilitation now puts it in an unusual position: a former ward of the state that is suddenly the subject of a major banking sector bid.

## Why It Matters

**The guidance raise is a negotiating move, not just a financial update.** By lifting its 2026 profit target, MPS management is signalling to Intesa, to the Italian Treasury, and to its own shareholders that the bank is worth more than any lowball offer would imply. It is a classic pre-bid manoeuvre: set the earnings bar higher, force the acquirer to pay up or look like it is stealing the asset. Whether the new targets are conservative or aggressive relative to actual operating trends is the question analysts will now need to answer.

**The Mediobanca failure changes the context significantly.** MPS had been the aggressor, launching an unsolicited approach for Mediobanca that ultimately went nowhere. That reversal leaves MPS without a clear M&A-driven growth story of its own, which makes it more vulnerable to an approach from a larger rival. Intesa, Italy's biggest bank by assets, would gain retail deposit funding and geographic coverage in central Italy. The logic is straightforward. The price is not.

**The Italian state's role adds a layer of political risk that does not exist in a typical bank deal.** Rome's residual shareholding means any agreed bid will need to satisfy the Treasury's return expectations, and Italian politicians have historically been reluctant to see domestic financial institutions fully absorbed without conditions. Intesa will likely need to offer job guarantees and regional commitments that could weigh on the synergy case. The government's willingness to sell, and at what price, may matter as much as the commercial terms.
