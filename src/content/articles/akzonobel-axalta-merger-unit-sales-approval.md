## What Happened

AkzoNobel and Axalta Coating Systems are closing in on regulatory approval for their proposed merger, with both companies agreeing to divest two business units to address antitrust concerns. The most significant of those is AkzoNobel's European refinishing unit, which the Dutch group is in active talks to sell, according to reporting from the Wall Street Journal. The divestitures are designed to satisfy competition authorities who flagged overlaps in the vehicle refinishing segment, where the two companies are among the largest global suppliers.

The combination would create one of the world's biggest coatings businesses, with operations spanning automotive, industrial, and decorative paint markets across dozens of countries. Neither company has disclosed the financial terms of the asset sales, but the refinishing unit is a meaningful business, and the willingness to sell it signals how much both sides want this deal through.

## Why It Matters

**Coatings consolidation has been years in the making.** The global paints and coatings market is dominated by a small number of players, and AkzoNobel has been under pressure from shareholders to improve margins and scale since at least 2017, when it rebuffed a takeover approach from PPG Industries. A merger with Axalta, which was previously owned by Berkshire Hathaway and is backed by Carlyle Group, would give AkzoNobel a significantly larger footprint in the automotive coatings segment, where Axalta is particularly strong. For Axalta shareholders, it offers a premium exit from a stock that had underperformed in recent years.

**The divestiture strategy is now the critical path.** Regulators in Europe, and likely elsewhere, identified the vehicle refinishing market as the primary area of competitive concern. AkzoNobel's decision to put its European refinishing business on the block is a direct response to that. Selling two units rather than one suggests the remedy package is broader than initially expected, which implies the regulatory pushback was more pointed. The identity of the buyers matters too: if the divested assets end up with a well-capitalised competitor, regulators are more likely to be satisfied. A sale to a weaker or less credible operator could draw further scrutiny.

**The deal structure will shape the combined company's profile.** Stripping out European refinishing before closing changes the strategic logic of the merger to some degree. That business carries decent margins in a niche but stable market. Axalta's strength in automotive OEM coatings, combined with AkzoNobel's decorative and industrial businesses, still produces a compelling combination, but investors should track how much EBITDA walks out the door alongside the divested units. The net effect on leverage and earnings per share post-close is worth watching closely once deal terms are public.
