## What Happened

Surgery Partners has raised its buyout price for HealthSpan to $25 per share in an amended merger agreement, pushing the deal's total implied value to up to $6 billion. The revised terms represent a sweetened offer from Surgery Partners after, it appears, the original bid failed to generate enough support. No timeline for closing was specified in the available reporting, and the deal remains subject to shareholder and regulatory approval.

Surgery Partners operates a network of ambulatory surgery centres and surgical facilities across the United States. HealthSpan is a healthcare services business with enough scale to make this, at up to $6 billion, one of the larger transactions in the outpatient and ambulatory space in recent memory.

## Why It Matters

**A bump this size signals friction in the original process.** When an acquirer comes back with a higher number before a deal closes, it generally means one of two things: shareholder advisory firms raised objections, or enough individual holders signalled they would vote no. Either way, Surgery Partners clearly decided the deal was worth more to them than the original price suggested, which raises the question of how much of that incremental value is real synergy versus the cost of getting a deal done.

**The ambulatory surgery sector is consolidating fast.** Payors and health systems have spent years pushing procedures out of expensive inpatient settings and into outpatient facilities. That structural shift has made surgery centre networks attractive acquisition targets, and private equity has been active throughout the space. Surgery Partners, itself backed by Bain Capital, is trying to build scale before the competitive window narrows further. A deal at this valuation implies a premium that only makes sense if the combined entity can accelerate volume growth or extract meaningful back-office and contracting efficiencies.

**Regulatory scrutiny of healthcare consolidation has tightened.** The FTC has shown consistent willingness in recent years to challenge hospital and physician-group combinations, and while ambulatory surgery centres have faced somewhat less scrutiny than acute-care hospital mergers, a transaction of this size will get a close look. Geographic overlap in specific markets could attract conditions or divestitures even if outright prohibition is unlikely.
