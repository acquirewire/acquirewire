Deal Overview

Neurocrine Biosciences (NASDAQ: NBIX) announced on April 6 that it will acquire Soleno Therapeutics (NASDAQ: SLNO) for $53 per share in an all-cash tender offer, valuing Soleno at approximately $2.9 billion. The offer represents a 34% premium to Soleno's closing price on April 2 and a 51% premium to its 30-day volume-weighted average price. Soleno shares surged roughly 33% in premarket trading.

Neurocrine, a San Diego-based neuroscience company with $2.86 billion in 2025 revenue (driven almost entirely by its tardive dyskinesia drug Ingrezza), will fund the deal with its $2.54 billion cash pile supplemented by modest pre-payable debt. The transaction is expected to close within 90 days, subject to a majority tender and HSR clearance. Soleno's board unanimously recommends the deal. The merger agreement includes a $95.25 million termination fee payable by Soleno and a $141.5 million reverse termination fee payable by Neurocrine.

Why It Matters

The commercial proof point: Soleno's Vykat XR (diazoxide choline) was approved by the FDA in March 2025 as the first and only treatment for hyperphagia in Prader-Willi syndrome. In its first nine months on the market, the drug generated $190.4 million in net revenue, priced at $466,000 per patient per year. By year-end 2025, 859 patients were actively on drug, representing just 12.5% of the estimated U.S. addressable market. Neurocrine is buying a proven commercial asset with enormous room to run.

The rare disease playbook: Neurocrine already has a best-in-class rare disease sales force from its Ingrezza franchise. Layering Vykat XR onto that infrastructure is the kind of deal that pays for itself through distribution leverage alone, without needing to build a new commercial organization from scratch. The overlap in prescriber relationships (neurologists, endocrinologists) makes the fit tighter than a typical bolt-on.

The "surprising" price: Stifel analysts flagged the deal as unexpectedly cheap for Soleno shareholders, given the drug's trajectory. At roughly 15x trailing revenue for a product with 87.5% of its market still untapped, Soleno's board may face questions about whether it left money on the table. But for Neurocrine, this is a rare chance to buy a first-in-class orphan drug before peak penetration.

⚠ Risks to Watch

Payer resistance at scale: Vykat XR costs $466K/year. Coverage currently reaches 60% of U.S. lives, but pushing deeper into Medicaid and smaller plans will test pricing power.

Single-product dependency: Soleno has no meaningful pipeline beyond Vykat XR. If the drug hits a safety signal or competitive threat, there is no fallback.

Small patient population: PWS affects roughly 1 in 15,000 births. Even at full penetration, this is a niche market. The $2.9B price requires sustained pricing and adherence.

✓ Bull Case

87.5% of patients untouched: Only 12.5% of the U.S. addressable market has been reached in nine months. The penetration curve is still steep, with 630 prescribers already active and growing.

Orphan drug economics: First-in-class, no competition, 7 years of market exclusivity remaining. Neurocrine's existing rare disease infrastructure can accelerate growth without proportional cost increases.

Deal Overview

Goldman Sachs (NYSE: GS) closed its acquisition of Innovator Capital Management on April 2, 2026, in a transaction valued at approximately $2 billion in cash and equity, subject to performance targets. The deal was first announced in December 2025. Innovator, based in Wheaton, Illinois, is the pioneer of defined-outcome ETFs, a category that uses options-based strategies to offer investors downside protection in exchange for capped upside.

The acquisition adds Innovator's 171 ETFs and roughly $31 billion in assets under supervision (AUS) to Goldman Sachs Asset Management, which now manages approximately 240 ETFs globally with $90 billion in total ETF AUS. That positions Goldman as a top-ten global active ETF provider. Innovator's 70-plus employees have joined Goldman, with co-founders Bruce Bond and John Southard becoming Advisory Directors and CIO Graham Day and distribution head Trevor Terrell joining as Partners.

Why It Matters

Buy vs. build in asset management: Goldman tried for years to grow its ETF business organically. It worked, but not fast enough. Active ETFs pulled nearly a third of all U.S. ETF inflows in 2025, and defined-outcome strategies were among the hottest subsegments. Rather than spend five years building a competitive options-overlay platform, Goldman wrote a check. The $2 billion price tag, roughly 7.1% of AUM, is steep by asset management standards but buys immediate scale in a category with structural tailwinds.

The distribution arbitrage: Innovator built great products but distributed them through a 70-person team. Goldman distributes through one of the largest wealth management and institutional sales networks on the planet. The thesis is straightforward: put Innovator's defined-outcome ETFs in front of Goldman's client base and watch AUM compound. If Goldman can grow Innovator's assets to $50B within two years, the acquisition pays for itself on fee revenue alone.

The active ETF land grab: Global active ETF assets approached $1.8 trillion by year-end 2025, and over 85% of newly launched ETFs were actively managed. Goldman, BlackRock, JPMorgan, and Capital Group are all racing to build dominant active ETF platforms. This acquisition is Goldman's clearest declaration that it intends to win that race.

⚠ Risks to Watch

Integration complexity: Migrating 171 ETFs onto Goldman's fund accounting, NAV calculation, and compliance infrastructure without disrupting shareholders is operationally intensive. The next 90 days are critical.

Price premium: At 7.1% of AUM, Goldman paid well above typical asset management acquisition multiples. If active ETF flows slow or buffer strategies fall out of favor, the payback period extends uncomfortably.

✓ Bull Case

Distribution leverage: Goldman's global sales network selling Innovator's proven products is the textbook case for 1+1=3 in asset management M&A.

Structural demand: Defined-outcome ETFs serve the massive retirement and wealth preservation market. As rate volatility persists, demand for downside-buffered products should keep growing.

Deal Overview

Unilever (LON: ULVR) and McCormick & Company (NYSE: MKC) announced on March 31 that they have agreed to combine Unilever's global Foods business (excluding India) with McCormick in a Reverse Morris Trust transaction. The deal values Unilever Foods at $44.8 billion, corresponding to 13.8x 2025 EBITDA. The combined entity will be worth approximately $65 billion with $20 billion in annual revenue.

Under the terms, Unilever and its shareholders will receive 65% of the combined company's fully diluted equity (valued at $29.1 billion) plus $15.7 billion in cash. McCormick shareholders will own approximately 35%. McCormick CEO Brendan Foley will lead the combined company from its headquarters in Hunt Valley, Maryland, with an additional international headquarters in the Netherlands. McCormick has secured $15.7 billion in bridge financing from Citigroup, Goldman Sachs, and Morgan Stanley. Net leverage at close is expected to be 4.0x, with a target of 3.0x within two years.

The deal is the largest Reverse Morris Trust involving a European company and the second-largest food transaction in history.

Why It Matters

Unilever's strategic exit: CEO Fernando Fernandez has spent a year dismantling Unilever's conglomerate structure. The ice cream business (Ben & Jerry's, Magnum) was spun off in December. Now the food division follows. What remains is a focused household and personal care company, spanning Dove, Axe, and Domestos, with a faster growth profile and simpler story for investors. The $15.7 billion cash payment funds debt reduction and a massive 6.9 billion euro buyback program running through 2029.

McCormick's transformation: McCormick goes from a $7 billion spice company to a $20 billion global food platform overnight. Adding Hellmann's (the world's largest mayonnaise brand) and Knorr (stock cubes and seasonings dominant in 80+ countries) to its existing Frank's RedHot, Cholula, and French's portfolio creates a genuine flavor conglomerate with pricing power across retail and foodservice channels globally.

The market's skepticism: McCormick shares fell 9% and Unilever dropped 3% on the announcement. RBC analyst James Edward Jones captured the bear case: Unilever is giving up 100% ownership of two dominant brands (Knorr and Hellmann's) for a 55% stake in a sprawling food conglomerate. Investors wanted a cleaner exit, not a complex RMT structure that leaves Unilever entangled for years.

⚠ Risks to Watch

Leverage at 4.0x: McCormick is absorbing enormous debt to fund the cash component. A consumer spending slowdown or failed synergy capture could strain the balance sheet for years.

Integration scale: Merging two global food operations across dozens of countries, manufacturing facilities, and distribution networks is a multi-year project. The $600M synergy target is ambitious.

Regulatory gauntlet: A $65B food combination will face antitrust scrutiny in multiple jurisdictions. The mid-2027 timeline already assumes a long review process.

✓ Bull Case

Brand portfolio depth: Hellmann's, Knorr, McCormick, Frank's, Cholula, and Maille under one roof. These are category leaders with pricing power and global distribution that no competitor can replicate.

Foodservice scale: The combined company becomes a dominant supplier to restaurants and food manufacturers worldwide, with cross-selling opportunities that neither company could access alone.

Deal Overview

Eli Lilly (NYSE: LLY) announced on March 30 a definitive agreement to acquire Centessa Pharmaceuticals (NASDAQ: CNTA), a UK-based clinical-stage biotech, for $38 per share in cash (roughly $6.3 billion), representing a 37% premium to Centessa's closing price. Shareholders will also receive one non-transferable contingent value right (CVR) worth up to $9 per share, bringing the total potential deal value to approximately $7.8 billion.

The CVR pays out in three tranches: $2 per share upon FDA approval of cleminorexton or ORX142 for narcolepsy type 2, $5 per share upon approval for idiopathic hypersomnia, and $2 per share upon a first FDA approval in any indication before January 1, 2030. The transaction, structured as a scheme of arrangement under English law, is expected to close in Q3 2026. This is Lilly's third acquisition of 2026, following a $2.4 billion bid for Orna Therapeutics and the $1.2 billion Ventyx Bio deal, plus an $8.8 billion alliance with Innovent Biologics.

Why It Matters

The orexin thesis: Centessa's lead candidate, cleminorexton (formerly ORX750), is an orexin receptor 2 (OX2R) agonist designed to treat excessive daytime sleepiness. The drug has shown positive Phase IIa results across narcolepsy type 1, narcolepsy type 2, and idiopathic hypersomnia. Orexin agonists are considered the most promising new class of sleep-wake medications in decades, essentially the opposite of Merck's Belsomra (an orexin antagonist that induces sleep). Lilly is buying the potential to own the wakefulness side of that equation.

The GLP-1 windfall at work: Lilly's Mounjaro and Zepbound franchises are throwing off so much cash that the company can afford to spend $7.8 billion on a Phase II asset without blinking. Three acquisitions and a major licensing deal in three months signals a company that is deploying its GLP-1 profits aggressively to build the next generation of its pipeline. The CVR structure limits overpayment risk: Lilly pays full price only if the drugs actually get approved.

Sleep medicine is a massive market: Narcolepsy affects roughly 200,000 people in the U.S. alone, and idiopathic hypersomnia may affect even more. Current treatments (stimulants, sodium oxybate) are imperfect. A well-tolerated orexin agonist could capture billions in peak sales. Lilly is not just buying one drug; it is buying a platform (cleminorexton, ORX142, ORX489) that could generate multiple products across several sleep-wake indications.

⚠ Risks to Watch

Phase II risk: Cleminorexton has mid-stage data only. Phase III trials have not started, and registrational studies are not expected to begin until Q1 2027. A lot can go wrong between here and approval.

Competitive field: Takeda's TAK-861 and other orexin agonists are in development. If a competitor reaches the market first or shows better data, Lilly's $6.3 billion upfront could look expensive.

UK scheme of arrangement: The deal requires court approval and a 75% shareholder vote under English law. More complex and less certain than a standard U.S. tender offer.

✓ Bull Case

Platform, not a single asset: Cleminorexton, ORX142, and ORX489 give Lilly multiple shots on goal across narcolepsy, idiopathic hypersomnia, and potentially Parkinson's-related sleepiness.

CVR limits downside: $1.5 billion of the deal value is contingent on FDA approvals. If the drugs fail, Lilly keeps the $1.5B. If they succeed, the total price is still reasonable for a best-in-class franchise.

Deal Overview

Sysco Corporation (NYSE: SYY), the largest food distributor in the United States, announced on March 30 that it will acquire Jetro Restaurant Depot, the nation's largest cash-and-carry food wholesaler, for approximately $29.1 billion. Restaurant Depot shareholders will receive $21.6 billion in cash and 91.5 million Sysco shares. Based on Sysco's closing price of $81.80 on March 27, the total consideration represents 14.6x Restaurant Depot's operating income, or 13.0x including expected synergies.

Restaurant Depot, founded in Brooklyn in 1976, operates 166 large-format warehouse stores across 35 states. In calendar year 2025, it generated approximately $16 billion in revenue, $2.1 billion in EBITDA, and $1.9 billion in free cash flow, with a 30-year track record of uninterrupted EBITDA growth. The combined company generated nearly $100 billion in revenue and $6.4 billion in adjusted EBITDA in 2025.

Sysco plans to fund the cash portion with $21 billion in new debt and hybrid instruments plus $1 billion from cash and equity. Restaurant Depot shareholders will own roughly 16% of the combined company. Sysco is pausing share buybacks during the deleveraging period. The deal is expected to close by Q3 of Sysco's fiscal 2027, pending regulatory approval.

Why It Matters

The omnichannel bet: Sysco is a logistics company that delivers food to 700,000 customers. Restaurant Depot is a retailer where 725,000 independent operators drive to a warehouse and load their own trucks. These are fundamentally different business models. Sysco is betting that combining delivery-based distribution with cash-and-carry retail creates an omnichannel operation that can serve independent restaurants however they want to buy. It is a bold thesis. It may also be a naive one.

The FTC ghost: In 2015, a federal judge blocked Sysco's $3.5 billion acquisition of US Foods on antitrust grounds. That deal combined two broadline distributors. This deal is structurally different: Restaurant Depot is a cash-and-carry retailer, not a broadline competitor. Sysco will argue the businesses are complementary, not overlapping. But the Independent Restaurant Coalition has already called on the FTC to block the sale, and the combined entity's near-$100 billion in revenue will attract serious scrutiny.

Wall Street's verdict was instant: Sysco stock plunged 15% intraday on March 30, its worst single-day decline since the COVID crash. The objections are clear: $21 billion in new debt pushing pro forma leverage to roughly 4.5x, 19% share dilution, and a family-run Brooklyn warehouse business that has never operated inside a public company. The $250 million synergy target looks modest at 12.5% of Restaurant Depot's operating income, and investors are skeptical it can be achieved without disrupting the very culture that built a 30-year growth streak.

⚠ Risks to Watch

Leverage spike: Pro forma net debt-to-EBITDA jumps from 2.8x to approximately 4.5x. A recession or slowdown in restaurant spending would pressure Sysco's ability to deleverage on schedule.

Antitrust uncertainty: The FTC blocked Sysco's last major deal. While this acquisition is structurally different, a combined $100B revenue food company will receive intense regulatory scrutiny.

Cultural mismatch: Sysco is a fleet-based logistics corporation. Restaurant Depot is a family-run warehouse retailer. Blending those operating models without destroying what made Restaurant Depot successful is the hardest part of this deal.

✓ Bull Case

Financial profile: Restaurant Depot adds 45% more EBITDA and 55% more free cash flow to Sysco's base, with 30 years of uninterrupted growth. The business is a cash machine.

Procurement power: A $100B combined food purchasing operation creates massive supplier leverage. The $250M synergy target may prove conservative if procurement savings materialize as expected.

Independent restaurant access: Restaurant Depot's 725,000 customers are the most fragmented, hardest-to-reach segment of foodservice. Sysco just bought the largest direct relationship with that customer base in the country.
